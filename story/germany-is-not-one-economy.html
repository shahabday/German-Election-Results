Germany is Not One: The Economy

Thirty-five years after reunification, the East caught up on some measures and fell further behind on others. Eight economic indicators, animated year by year, on what money in Germany actually looks like depending on where you live.

↓ Scroll to begin
Income

Germany got richer. The gap narrowed, but never closed.

Average household income per resident has almost doubled everywhere since 2001, from €13,547 to €22,113 in the East, from €16,792 to €24,649 in the West. As a ratio, East German incomes climbed from 81% of the West's to 90% by 2021. But in real terms the gap is still about €2,500 a year, smaller than two decades ago, but not gone.

Economic output

Wealth is produced in a handful of cities, and it isn't evenly spread

GDP per capita has less to do with where people live than where they work. Wolfsburg (Volkswagen), Ingolstadt (Audi) and Munich top the ranking at well over €100,000 per resident, several times the national county median. Measured this way the East trails further behind than on almost any other metric here: 74% of the Western average in 2020, and the absolute gap has actually widened since 2000, from about €8,900 to €10,350.

Poverty

A household in the East is still a third more likely to be low-income

The share of households living on less than 60% of the median income has fallen everywhere since 2013, from 39.6% to 32.1% in the East, from 29.7% to 24.5% in the West. The relative gap has barely moved: East German households are still roughly 30% more likely to fall into this bracket than Western ones, in 2013 and in 2021 alike.

Labor market

The one gap that actually closed

In 1998 East German unemployment ran more than double the Western rate: 18.1% against 9.0%. By 2020 it was 6.7% against 5.2%, still higher, but within a point and a half for the first time since reunification. Of every metric in this story, unemployment shows the clearest, steadiest convergence.

Migration & the workforce

The workforce that never arrived in the East

Foreign nationals made up just 1.6% of the East's population in 1995, against 8.8% in the West, and the gap has barely closed since: 5.1% versus 12.5% by 2020. In an economy short of workers almost everywhere, the East has drawn a much smaller share of the migration that's filled gaps in the Western labor market. Berlin is the exception, climbing from 12.3% to 19.6% over the same period.

Cost of living

Rent is where the gap is still widening, not closing

Average asking rent has risen everywhere since 2010, but faster in the West: from €3.27/m² to €6.01 there, from €2.54 to €3.86 in the East. The ratio between them has widened, not narrowed, East rents now sit at 64% of Western levels, down from 78% in 2010. The most expensive places in the country aren't a region, they're a cluster: Munich and its wealthy commuter belt, plus Frankfurt and Stuttgart.

Housing

The real housing squeeze isn't East vs West, it's big city vs everywhere else

Living space per resident has grown everywhere since 2011, East and West alike, and the gap between them has narrowed to about three square meters. The tightest squeeze in the country runs through its biggest cities regardless of which Germany they're in: Frankfurt, Stuttgart, Berlin and Freiburg in the West, Rostock and Dresden in the East, all under 40 m² per resident while the national county average is nearly 50.

Inequality

The poorer half of the country is also the more equal half

By the Gini coefficient, Western states have run consistently more unequal than Eastern ones every year since 2021, an average of about 30 against 26. It's a genuine twist on the rest of this story: the East is poorer on almost every measure here, but its income is also more evenly spread. Inequality in Germany tracks with wealth and big cities, not with the East-West divide itself.

Germany is Not One: The Economy

The country got richer. Whether that closed the divide depends which number you look at.

Unemployment converged sharply. Income narrowed the gap by a third. But GDP per capita and rent are drifting further apart, not closer, and inequality runs in the opposite direction from the poverty gap entirely.
