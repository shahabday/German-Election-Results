Income and the Vote: Poor, Rich and Super-Rich States

Group states by average income instead of by geography, and a real pattern shows up in the AfD numbers — but not the simple 'poorer states vote AfD' story you might expect. What actually separates these groups is history as much as income.

↓ Scroll to begin
Three income tiers

Grouping states by income, not geography

Poor: Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Brandenburg, Sachsen, Sachsen-Anhalt and Niedersachsen. Rich: NRW and Bayern. Super-rich: Hessen and Baden-Württemberg. Each group's line below is an equal-weighted average of its member states' own AfD share.

The poor states

By far the strongest AfD support

The poor-state average was still at 0% before 2013, but reached 37.0% by 2025 — more than any single income group, and more than most individual states in this series.

The rich states

A much smaller rise

NRW and Bayern together reached just 19.8% by 2025 — almost exactly half the poor-state average, for the same election.

The super-rich states

Not actually the lowest

Here's the surprise: Hessen and Baden-Württemberg — Germany's two highest-income states — landed at 21.4%, slightly higher than the merely 'rich' NRW/Bayern group (19.8%), not lower. Income alone doesn't cleanly predict AfD support among the western states.

What actually explains the gap

It's East vs. West more than rich vs. poor

The real dividing line in this data isn't income tier — it's whether a state was part of the former East Germany. Four of the five 'poor' states here are East German; strip out Niedersachsen (which, at roughly the national-average income level, doesn't statistically belong in this group at all) and the remaining four East states would show an even higher average.

One correlation, one caveat

Income tracks the story, it doesn't drive it

Real average income (verfügbares Einkommen) does correlate with this chart's groupings — roughly €25,100 per person in Sachsen-Anhalt versus €31,500 in Bayern — but the East/West political history behind those numbers is doing at least as much explanatory work as income itself.

How these numbers are computed

Each line is an equal-weighted average of its member states' own "current standing" composites (federal, state and European results, unweighted municipality average within each state) — not a population-weighted national figure. Niedersachsen's inclusion in the "poor" group follows the grouping as given, but real income data (Statistisches Bundesamt, 2023) puts its disposable income per capita at roughly €28,000 — close to the national average of €28,452, and well above Sachsen-Anhalt's €25,094. Niedersachsen is solidly middle-income, not among Germany's poorest states by that measure; its presence in this "poor" group pulls that line's AfD average down slightly relative to the four East German states alone.
